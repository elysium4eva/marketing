Chapter outline

Part 1 · 2–20Marketing data and measurement: the four questions of marketing analytics, five data sources, questionnaire design (from question to item, item types and wording traps), probability and non-probability sampling, modes of administration and mode effects, reliability, validity, measurement error and common method bias, non-response and weighting — ending on the error chain that sets the ceiling for everything later
Part 2 · 21–40Conjoint analysis and discrete choice: valuing the bundle, the four design steps, full-factorial versus orthogonal versus D-optimal designs, effect coding and part-worths, individual-level preferences and their limits; then why choice models beat ratings, the three kinds of choice data, random utility and the logit derivation, binary and multinomial logit, the IIA problem with nested and mixed logit, an EV-policy example, and how a badly built choice set corrupts the coefficients
Part 3 · 41–52Hedonic prices and response models: decomposing a product into attributes, the semi-log specification, a housing regression with a quantile check, three uses of hedonic prices; then marketing spend to sales response, four functional forms, adstock carryover, promotion response split into baseline and increment, and what the three models share
Part 4 · 53–64Consumer data, journeys and attribution: clickstream sessions and the funnel, CRM and RFM, the five-stage journey, the four heuristic attribution rules and why last click is overrated, Shapley and Markov attribution on one dataset, privacy after the cookie, identity resolution and privacy-enhancing technologies
Part 5 · 65–76Recommendation, pricing and promotion: recommender problem definition and evaluation, collaborative filtering, matrix factorisation and ALS, precision@k, sparsity and cold start, deep and sequential models; then price elasticity, log-log estimation and profit-maximising price, discount, coupon and threshold mechanics, and the ethics and compliance of price discrimination
Part 6 · 77–90Churn, CLV and experiments: defining churn and its label window, the expected-loss threshold and its ROC, three ways to compute CLV, retention curves and discounting, the value × churn four-quadrant matrix, A/B design, two-proportion tests, power curves, CUPED, the difference between lift and increment, marketing-mix models and causality, and what changed in 2026
Part 7 · 91–100Tools, glossary and close: the tool stack, a marketing data and compliance checklist, ten common mistakes, the links to the four projects, an 85-term glossary, references, a one-page recap, ten self-check questions and a wrap-up

Learning objectives

  • Design a survey and a measurement instrument that support inference — population, frame, item wording, mode, weighting — and separate reliability from validity;
  • Run a conjoint study end to end: choose the study type, specify attributes and levels, build a fractional-factorial or D-optimal design, and interpret part-worths and attribute importance;
  • Explain random utility, write the logit choice probability, derive willingness to pay, and state what the IIA assumption rules out;
  • Estimate a hedonic price equation, read semi-elasticities, and use the fit to build a quality-adjusted price index;
  • Build a funnel, split credit across touchpoints with heuristic and data-driven attribution, and say why the split is a decision rule rather than a measurement;
  • Evaluate a recommender with the right metric, estimate price elasticity, and set a price or a promotion that maximises contribution rather than revenue;
  • Define churn and CLV operationally, choose a cost-weighted decision threshold, and design an experiment with enough power — then check incrementality rather than trusting the lift;
  • Judge when each method is the right tool, and when the available variation cannot support it.

Why this lecture matters for the project

The final project requires at least one quantitative method with an explicit design. This lecture supplies the candidates — survey, conjoint, choice model, hedonic regression, response model, attribution, recommender, elasticity, churn model, CLV, A/B test — and, more importantly, the habit of stating the identifying variation before reporting the estimate.
