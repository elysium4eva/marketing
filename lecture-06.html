Lecture outline

Part 1 · 5–13Survey design: what survey methodology is, inference and representativeness, the anatomy of a survey, four kinds of question, survey error and cost, the seven methodological decisions, modes of administration and mode effects
Part 2 · 14–23Conjoint analysis: what it does, part-worths and market-share models, the four design steps (study type, attributes, levels, fractional-factorial questionnaire), a worked laptop example, reading part-worths, advantages and disadvantages
Part 3 · 24–37Choice models: discrete versus continuous choice, random utility and McFadden, individual versus aggregate demand data, an applied NEV-subsidy example, binomial and multinomial models, the choice set, logit probabilities, utility, binary and multinomial examples, the IIA problem and the models that relax it
Part 4 · 38–42Hedonic regression: the decomposition logic, real-estate valuation, the hedonic equation and quality-adjusted price indices, platform data sources (Lianjia, Airbnb, Booking/Ctrip), applications beyond real estate
Part 5 · 43–47Response models: what a response model is, the three questions it answers, four insights (concavity, elasticity, allocation, competition), the concave response curve, and budget allocation by equalising marginal return
Part 6 · 48–50Practice: reading these models critically (identifying variation, substitution assumptions, counterfactual range) and a summary

Learning objectives

  • Design a survey that supports inference — population, frame, instrument, mode, weighting — and name the main sources of survey error;
  • Run a conjoint study end to end: choose the study type, specify attributes and levels, build a fractional-factorial design, and interpret part-worths;
  • Explain random utility, write the logit choice probability, derive willingness to pay, and state what the IIA assumption rules out;
  • Estimate a hedonic price equation and use it to construct a quality-adjusted price index;
  • Read a response model: recognise concavity, read elasticity, and allocate a budget by equalising marginal return per yuan;
  • Judge when each of the five methods is the right tool — and when the available variation cannot support it.

Why this lecture matters for the project

The final project requires at least one quantitative method with an explicit design. This lecture supplies the five candidates — survey, conjoint, choice model, hedonic regression, response model — and, more importantly, the habit of stating the identifying variation before reporting the estimate.
