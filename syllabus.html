2. Course Description

This module is designed for undergraduate and postgraduate students in international marketing. It introduces, systematically, how big data is sourced, prepared, modelled, evaluated and applied. The course follows the spine of data → model → optimisation → evaluation → deployment: it teaches the principles of statistics and machine learning while insisting on hands-on work with real business data. Every lecture keeps the code, sample data and figures fully reproducible.

The 2026 edition goes beyond networks and software tools and updates the material on generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) and agentic analytics across the board: from foundation models and reasoning models to retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) and tool calling, together with compute cost, evaluation reliability, data compliance and AI regulation. Students should come away understanding that model capability is being commoditised, and that the value now lies in data governance, verification workflows and business deployment.

The module runs across three applied tracks: commercial data, financial data, and marketing data (including cross-border trade data). Each student chooses one track and completes an end-to-end research project that runs through data acquisition, cleaning, exploration, modelling, causal evaluation and presentation of results.

The course places equal weight on academic standards and responsible use of artificial intelligence: any work assisted by a large language model (LLM) must declare, in the report, the tools used, the prompts and the way the output was verified by a human, and must comply with the rules on data copyright, privacy and content labelling.

3. Learning Outcomes

On completion of the module, students should be able to:

  • Explain the big-data ecosystem, data pipelines and the modern data stack (lakehouse, vector databases, feature stores), and set up a reproducible analysis environment;
  • Design a data-sourcing plan and complete data cleaning and quality assessment, identifying missing-data mechanisms, outliers and sample bias;
  • Use descriptive statistics and visualisation methods appropriately, and communicate analytical conclusions clearly to a non-technical audience;
  • Build supervised and unsupervised learning models, split data correctly, evaluate performance and guard against data leakage;
  • Understand in outline how deep learning, natural language processing and foundation models work, and how text and multimodal data enter marketing analysis;
  • Design and evaluate experiments (A/B tests, incrementality measurement) in marketing settings and distinguish correlation from causation;
  • Pose data-answerable research questions in marketing and cross-border commercial settings and deliver data-driven solutions;
  • Use AI tools responsibly (declaration, verification, reproducibility) and comply with data-ethics and regulatory requirements.

5. Chapter Details

Lecture 1 — Introduction: Big Data Analytics and the AI Landscape

  • Objectives: build a map of the course; understand the levels and the workflow of big-data analytics; get to grips with the current state of AI in terms of models, compute, regulation and the labour market.
  • Content: DIKW and the 5 V; levels of analytics (descriptive, diagnostic, predictive, prescriptive, plus generative and agentic analytics); the data pipeline and the modern data stack; machine learning 1-2-3; the 2022–2026 timeline and model comparisons; reasoning models and test-time compute; agentic AI and MCP; RAG; the evaluation crisis and hallucination mitigation; compute and energy; data copyright and synthetic data; the EU AI Act and China's AIGC labelling rules; enterprise adoption and ROI, and evidence from the labour market.
  • Tools and data: Kaggle, open datasets, a Python environment; LLM assistants (use declared).

Lecture 2 — Data Sourcing: Foundations of Data Science

  • Objectives: recognise data types and structures; master the channels for acquiring data and the methods of primary data collection; understand measurement error and social context.
  • Content: numeric/categorical, cross-section/time-series/panel, network data; structured and unstructured data; data sources (internal proprietary, open data, third-party DaaS, APIs, scraping); questionnaires, interviews, card sorting, experiments and A/B tests; measurement and precision; 2026 updates: data contracts, open table formats, vector databases and embeddings, data compliance and copyright, synthetic data, the legal and ethical limits of scraping.
  • Tools and data: Python (requests, BeautifulSoup, Scrapy, pandas), Kaggle, WITS/Comtrade, Wind.

Lecture 3 — Data Preparation: Cleaning and Quality

  • Objectives: merge and reshape data from multiple sources; clean data along the quality dimensions; handle missing values and outliers correctly.
  • Content: the five data-quality characteristics (validity, accuracy, completeness, consistency, uniqueness); append/merge/join and the various join types; the five-step cleaning recipe; missing-data mechanisms and imputation strategies; outlier detection (IQR, Z-score, isolation forest); data transformation and standardisation; 2026 updates: reproducible pipelines (dbt, Great Expectations), feature stores, LLM-assisted cleaning with human verification, anonymisation and privacy-enhancing technologies, data-drift monitoring.
  • Tools and data: pandas, Polars, DuckDB, OpenRefine, SQL.

Lecture 4 — Data Description and Visualization

  • Objectives: characterise data with descriptive statistics; choose the chart type that fits the question; build interactive dashboards and avoid misleading them.
  • Content: documentation and metadata standards; univariate, bivariate and multivariate statistics; expressing distributions, correlation and uncertainty; the grammar of graphics and visualisation principles; hands-on work with Power BI and Tableau; 2026 updates: AI-assisted charting and chart interpretation, spotting misleading charts, colour-blind-friendly and accessible design, narrative visualisation and data storytelling.
  • Tools and data: matplotlib, seaborn, plotly, ggplot2, Power BI, Tableau Public.

Lecture 5 — Introduction to Machine Learning

  • Objectives: distinguish the learning paradigms; understand bias–variance and evaluation protocols; build and evaluate a baseline model.
  • Content: the definition of machine learning and its applications in business and marketing; supervised, unsupervised, self-supervised and reinforcement learning; overfitting and regularisation; data splitting and cross-validation; classification and regression metrics; 2026 updates: the trade-off between AutoML and fine-tuning, interpretability, MLOps and model cards, a data-leakage checklist, and the cost and latency of models.
  • Tools and data: scikit-learn, XGBoost/LightGBM, MLflow, Weights & Biases.

Lecture 6 — Big Data in Marketing

  • Objectives: master the collection of marketing data and the design of experiments; use conjoint analysis, recommendation and customer-value models to support decisions.
  • Content: questionnaire design and measurement (reliability and validity, sampling and administration modes); conjoint analysis (attribute and level design, estimation, individual-level preferences); consumer data (clickstream, CRM, social media); the customer journey and attribution; recommender systems (collaborative filtering, matrix factorisation, deep recommendation); pricing and promotion; churn prediction and customer lifetime value (CLV); A/B and incrementality experiments; 2026 updates: generative marketing content and brand safety, the new privacy regime (the end of third-party cookies, privacy-enhancing technologies), conversational commerce, marketing mix models (MMM) combined with causal inference, and multimodal content analysis (image/video/livestream).
  • Tools and data: Qualtrics, Python (pandas, statsmodels, scikit-learn, LightFM), experiment-platform data.

6. Assessment

The final grade is 10% presentation + 90% project. The project follows a "choose 3 of 4" rule: students complete any three of the four research projects below. Compared with earlier editions the projects are more open — the topic and the data may be chosen freely, and the use of AI tools is encouraged (declared and verified as required by Section 10). The detailed project briefs and submission requirements will be announced in class (to be announced). Teams are limited to two students.

Project 1 — Data sourcing and preparationChoose data from any domain (commercial, financial, trade or marketing) and carry out data acquisition, a quality audit, cleaning and exploration, delivering a reproducible data pipeline and a data description.
Project 2 — Predictive modellingBuild a supervised learning model on data of your choice (linear model, tree ensemble or neural network), with feature engineering, cross-validation, performance evaluation and error analysis, and interpret the key features.
Project 3 — Text and foundation-model applicationsUse NLP or a large language model for a text analysis task (sentiment, topic, information extraction or question answering), including a self-built evaluation set, hallucination checks and guardrail design, and a declaration of the models and prompts used.
Project 4 — Causal and applied analysisFor a policy or business intervention question, design an identification strategy (experiment, DID, IV, RDD or matching), estimate the effect and discuss the assumptions and robustness; or complete an integrated analysis in a financial, trade or marketing setting.
Project briefs (to be announced)The specific topics, data, tasks and marking scheme for the four projects will be announced in class; the projects are more open than in previous editions, allowing freely chosen data and research questions, and the use of AI tools is encouraged (declaration and verification required).

Past Project Reports (for topic choice and style) ↗

Report requirements: problem and background, data and sources, method and code, results and interpretation, limitations and robustness, reproduction notes, and an AI-use declaration; the main text should not exceed 10 pages, with code and data submitted separately.

7. Project Tracks and Topics

Module 1 — Commercial and Consumer Data

  • Churn prediction and retention strategy; marketing mix models and incrementality measurement; recommender systems and personalisation; price elasticity and evaluation of promotion effectiveness.

Module 2 — Financial Data

  • Sentiment in earnings text and return prediction; credit-default prediction and model interpretability; graph analysis for fraud detection; portfolio optimisation and risk measurement; LLM research assistants and RAG evaluation.

Module 3 — Marketing and International Trade Data

  • Conjoint analysis and preference measurement; attribution and customer lifetime value; gravity models and the estimation of trade potential; evaluating the effects of trade policy (tariffs and agreements); global value chains and input–output analysis; cross-border e-commerce platform analytics.

Topic note: students may propose their own topic, but it must relate to the lecture content of this module. A one-page topic note (problem, data, method, expected output) must be submitted — see the Week 5 deliverable in the schedule.

10. Course Policies

  • Attendance and participation: attendance is recorded and class discussion and hands-on work are expected throughout; unexcused absence is penalised per session (cumulative absence of more than one third of the sessions is handled under university regulations). The 10% presentation component is assessed in class.
  • Deadlines and late submission: submissions up to 24 hours after a project deadline lose 5%, and a further 5% for each additional 24 hours, up to a maximum deduction of 15%; please give advance notice of special circumstances.
  • Academic integrity: plagiarism, ghost-writing, and fabrication of data or results are prohibited; any data, code or text taken from others must be properly cited.
  • AI-use policy: LLM assistance for study, coding and analysis is allowed, but you must (1) declare the tool name and version; (2) keep the key prompts; (3) verify the model output by hand; and (4) never submit unverified conclusions or code. Undeclared use is treated as academic misconduct.
  • Data and privacy: data containing personal information must be anonymised, and applicable law and platform terms must be respected; restricted data must not be uploaded to public model services.
  • Communication and office hours: email (Xieer.dai@szu.edu.cn) or an appointment in person (Mingde Building 1310); course announcements are made on the course platform.

Appendix: AI-Use Declaration Template

Tools: name / version / date of use.

Purpose: code writing / text editing / data analysis / chart generation (itemised).

Key prompts: list at least three of the main prompts.

Verification: for example "recomputed the key statistics with pandas", "checked 30 model outputs by hand", "compared the API usage against the official documentation".

Parts done without AI: state which parts were completed entirely independently.
